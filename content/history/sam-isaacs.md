# Sam Isaacs

Sam Isaacs was an Aboriginal stockman employed by the Bussell family at Wallcliffe,
near the mouth of the Margaret River. On the morning of 1 December 1876 he saw the
SS Georgette going ashore in Calgardup Bay, and what he did next is the reason this
exhibition exists.

## What happened

Isaacs saw the wreck first. He rode to the Bussell homestead to raise the alarm,
and it was Grace Bussell — sixteen years old — who insisted on riding back to the
beach with him. Not the other way around.

By one family account, passed down through Isaacs' own descendants, it was Isaacs
who went into the surf again and again, hauling survivors clear. Grace Bussell, after
her horse struggled to hold its footing and Isaacs warned her she risked drowning
the very people she was trying to save, returned to the beach and stayed there.

and it should be attributed as precisely as the family are comfortable with — who

evidence. An unattributed one reads as speculation, and the whole argument of this
page depends on it not reading that way.

## What each of them received

For his part in the rescue, Isaacs was awarded the Royal Humane Society's **bronze**
medal.

Grace Bussell received the **silver** medal, a gold watch and chain paid for by
public subscription, a place in the history books, and a town named after her.

Isaacs received a smaller medal, no land, no watch, and — in most tellings since —
a supporting role in someone else's story.

## The land at Wallcliffe

Isaacs' hundred acres at Wallcliffe are often folded into the rescue story as
though the land were part of the same reward.

It was not. He bought it himself, in 1897 — twenty-one years after the rescue — at
full price. In doing so he became the first Aboriginal person in Western Australia
to hold a freehold Crown grant.

That was his own doing, not a gift, and describing it as a reward gets the fact
exactly backwards while also taking the achievement away from him.

[SOURCE NEEDED] The Crown grant record. This is a checkable primary document and,
given how often the claim is repeated incorrectly, the citation is worth having on

## Why the record reads the way it does

Marcia van Zeller, whose research into the Georgette formed the basis of her
doctoral novel *The Capes* (Curtin University, 2014), has argued that Sam Isaacs'
contribution was systematically underplayed.

Not, on her account, by any single act of erasure — but by the accumulated weight
of a culture that found Grace Bussell's story more convenient, more romantic, and
more publishable. A sixteen-year-old white girl on a horse could be compared to
Grace Darling. An Aboriginal stockman could not be fitted into that shape, so the
story was told without him at the centre of it, and each retelling moved him a
little further out.

Van Zeller gives a free public talk during the exhibition:

## Isaac Rock

The rock standing off the Calgardup Bay shoreline was known as **Black Rock** at the
time of the wreck. Today it is called **Isaac Rock**.
