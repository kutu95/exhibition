# Captain John Godfrey

John Godfrey was master of the SS Georgette on her last voyage, and the man the
[Court of Inquiry](/history/court-of-inquiry-1876) held responsible for her loss.

He is also the only person in this story whom visitors to the exhibition can
speak to: the [Captain Godfrey installation](/installations/captain-godfrey) is an
interactive MetaHuman built from the inquiry records.

## The decisions

Three moments decided the fate of the ship, and Godfrey was present for all of
them.

**The evening of 30 November.** Rounding Cape Naturaliste, the Georgette began to
leak. The Chief Engineer reported extra water in the bilge. Godfrey was informed.
He ordered the pumps checked — and did not go below himself to look.

**Shortly after midnight.** Midway between Cape Naturaliste and Cape Hamelin, the
pumps failed outright. By 4am, passengers and crew were bailing by bucket and
losing ground.

**Near dawn.** The rising water reached the boilers and put out the fires. With a
flooding hull and no engines, Godfrey turned the ship for the beach. On this point
the record is not really in dispute: it was the only decision left that offered
anyone a chance of survival.

## The lifeboat

In the darkness before dawn, with the ship going down, Godfrey ordered the lifeboat
and the gig launched.

Twenty-two people were placed in the lifeboat — men, women and children, some with
infants in arms. The intention was that it would be towed astern until the ship
grounded and a safer landing could be arranged.

The gig, with fourteen aboard, made it ashore at Injidup, miles to the north. The
lifeboat never reached the shore at all. As it was lowered it stove in against the
hull and capsized.

## The verdict

The marine inquiry sat in the small Busselton courtroom in December 1876. Godfrey
was found guilty and his certificate was suspended.

He never accepted the verdict.

## Why he is worth taking seriously

It is easy to read the sequence — a leak reported, pumps ordered checked, a captain
who did not go below — as straightforward negligence, and the court did.

But the harder question, and the one the installation puts to visitors directly, is
what a competent master would have done differently at each point with the
information actually available to him at the time. Beaching a sinking ship on a
surf coast in darkness is not a mistake; it is what you do when the alternative is
foundering in deep water.

The exhibition does not argue that the verdict was wrong. It argues that a verdict
is not the same thing as an explanation.
