# Grace Bussell

Grace Bussell was sixteen years old, living at the Wallcliffe homestead near the
mouth of the Margaret River, when the SS Georgette came ashore in Calgardup Bay on
1 December 1876.

This page is not an attempt to take the story away from her. It is an attempt to
say what is actually known.

## The version that spread

The press at the time credited Grace Bussell with riding repeatedly into the surf
to pull survivors to safety. The story spread through the colony and reached
London. She was compared to Grace Darling, the English lighthouse keeper's daughter
celebrated for her own sea rescue nearly forty years earlier.

Medals were struck. Paintings were made. A town was named after her. The legend was
set within months and has held for a century and a half.

## The account of a man who was there

Passenger George Leake — a young law student who had been on board and narrowly
escaped drowning — left his own, quieter account, which contradicts parts of the
published story.

By his telling:

- The horses could not have kept their footing in surf that heavy.
- Much of the landing had already been managed by the crew, using a rope system
  hauled between the ship and the beach, before the riders arrived.

He was at pains to say that Grace Bussell had behaved admirably, and would have
gone further into the water had it been necessary. But what he described her
actually doing was a far quieter thing than the legend required.

> "The vessel was seen going ashore by one of Mr Bussell's stockmen, and he and one
> of the Miss Bussells came down to us on the beach; it was a great relief to see
> them, for then we knew help was near."
>
> — George Leake, 1877

Two things in that sentence are worth noticing. The stockman — [Sam
Isaacs](/history/sam-isaacs) — saw the wreck and is unnamed. And the relief Leake
describes is the relief of knowing help was coming, not of being pulled from the
water.

## What she was given

Grace Bussell received the Royal Humane Society's silver medal, a gold watch and
chain paid for by public subscription, and a town named after her.

Sam Isaacs, who saw the wreck first and rode for help, received the bronze medal.

## Holding both things at once

A sixteen-year-old rode a horse down to a beach where people were drowning, at some
real risk to herself, and by every account behaved well. That is true.

It is also true that the story printed about her was not the story the surviving
passengers told, that the man who raised the alarm was written down to a supporting
role, and that the colony preferred the version it got.

The exhibition's position is that the second set of facts does not diminish the
first. Grace Bussell did not build the legend. She was sixteen. What was made of
her afterwards was made by other people.
