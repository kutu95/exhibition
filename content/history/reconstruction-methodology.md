# How this history was reconstructed

This page exists because the exhibition makes claims that differ from the accepted
version of the Georgette story — most importantly about [Sam
Isaacs](/history/sam-isaacs) — and anyone encountering those claims is entitled to
know how they were arrived at.

It also states plainly where the reconstruction stops and interpretation begins.
That distinction matters more than usual here, because part of this project is
built with AI.

## Sources

**Primary records.** Lloyd's Register entries for the ship's construction and
particulars; contemporary newspaper accounts; the transcript of the [marine inquiry
held at Busselton in December 1876](/history/court-of-inquiry-1876).

**A surviving passenger's account.** George Leake, a law student aboard the
Georgette who narrowly escaped drowning, wrote his own account in 1877 which
contradicts parts of the published press version of the rescue.

**Institutional archives.** Marcia van Zeller's research for her doctoral novel
*The Capes* (Curtin University, 2014) drew on the State Records Office of Western
Australia and the Battye Library. The inquiry transcript is held at the State Records
Office; George Leake's 1877 letter is at the Battye Library.

**Oral history.** An account passed down through Sam Isaacs' own descendants, which
differs materially from the newspaper version. [SOURCE NEEDED — attribution.]

**The site itself.** Eight years of photography at Calgardup Bay, Redgate Beach and
Isaac Rock, including drone survey of the wreck on the rare days it is visible.
This is where the project has something no archive holds: a repeated visual record
of the wreck site across seasons and conditions.

## Method

The approach was to treat the press account as evidence about the colony rather
than as evidence about the beach.

Where the newspapers, the Leake account and the descendant account disagree, the
exhibition does not average them. It sets them beside each other and asks which is
better placed to know. On the question of whether horses could hold their footing
in that surf, a passenger standing in the water outranks a journalist in Perth.

Where the record is simply silent — and it is silent about a great deal — the
exhibition says so rather than filling the gap.

## Where AI was used, and where it was not

This needs stating precisely, because "AI reconstruction of a historical figure" is
a phrase that should make people cautious.

**[Captain Godfrey](/installations/captain-godfrey)** is an interactive MetaHuman
that visitors can speak with. He is built from the inquiry records: the evidence
given, the decisions examined, the verdict returned. The system is constrained to
that material.

He is a *reconstruction*, not a recording. He does not know things the record does
not contain, and where visitors ask him something the record cannot answer, the
honest response is that it cannot be answered. Anything he says beyond the
documents is interpretation, and should be treated as such.

**The photographs are photographs.** No part of the photographic work is
AI-generated. AI was used to build tools — a compositing tool, a four-wall video
editor, the [Drift](/installations/drift) motion system — not images.

One exception is labelled as such: the broken propeller image used on the book
cover is an artist's impression based on surviving photographs, not a photograph of
the propeller.

## What is still unresolved

An honest list, kept current:

- **Seven or eight drowned in the lifeboat.** [The site currently says both.
  Resolve against the inquiry record.]
- **Whether the rescuers gave evidence** to the Court of Inquiry.
- **When and why Black Rock became Isaac Rock**, and whether the renaming was
  formal.
- **Where the broken propeller lies**, and what became of the other one.
- **The grounds on which Captain Godfrey rejected the verdict.**
